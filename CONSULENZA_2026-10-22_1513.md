# Consulenza Astra — 2026-10-22 15:13 UTC

Costo reale: $0.0919 (981 in / 3481 out, tariffa flex)

## Verdict

**Do not run this design as written.** It fixes the original denominator error—judging wallets only within sampled tokens—but can still produce a convincing, uncopyable false winner.

You need three separate tests:

1. **Accounting:** Did this economic entity actually increase its wealth?
2. **Persistence:** Could you identify it *before* its subsequent profits occurred?
3. **Execution:** Could your follower capture those subsequent profits at executable prices, after costs, with your capital?

A wallet can pass the first and fail both others. Your proposed design blurs them.

## 1. Specific failures in the design

### A. An address’s signatures are not necessarily its complete economic history

`getSignaturesForAddress(wallet)` is an index of transactions mentioning that address, not a complete beneficial-owner ledger.

Examples:

- Someone transfers tokens into the wallet’s associated token account without mentioning the owner address.
- A delegate or relayer executes an economically relevant transaction.
- Inventory moves between an operator’s wallets before being sold.
- Trading continues through a migrated pool or another venue.

Skipping relayer transactions avoids a false attribution, but **does not make wallet-level P&L complete**. It can create systematic omissions.

The 22/22 agreement and round-trip calibration are useful validations of the transactions you read. They do not validate coverage of everything you must read.

**Fix:** Reconcile opening balances, closing balances, trades, and external transfers for every ranked entity. Inspect relevant token accounts when reconciliation fails. Mark unresolved histories **unrankable**, not profitable.

### B. “SOL received minus SOL spent” is not realised profit

Depending on exactly what you count, it is either:

- net cash flow from trading, with all unsold inventory implicitly marked at zero; or
- a mixture of trading, deposits, withdrawals, account rent, tips, transfers, and unrelated activity.

A deposit is not profit. A withdrawal is not a loss. Selling tokens acquired before your observation window is not free money.

**Fix:** Define the ledger and inventory boundaries before pulling histories. More below.

### C. “Whole visible history” creates unequal and potentially truncated observation windows

You will encounter:

- wallets whose acquisition history is outside provider coverage;
- very active wallets too expensive to finish;
- wallets with missing acquisitions;
- older wallets with much more opportunity to earn absolute SOL;
- recent wallets with positions not yet resolved.

If you stop reading expensive histories and still rank them, your budget becomes part of the selection mechanism.

**Fix:** Use explicit calendar windows and declared coverage rules. “Complete history” is not a requirement you can quietly relax when credits run low.

### D. Splitting every wallet into two halves is not a clean persistence test

It fails if:

- wallets are selected using full-history profits or activity;
- the split is by trade count and wallets therefore face different market periods;
- you adjust filters after seeing the second half;
- entities are defined retrospectively in ways that affect selection;
- positions straddle the boundary without correct opening inventory.

Both halves can also contain the same coordinated campaign or exceptional token.

**Fix:** Use common calendar cutoffs:

- **Discovery period:** seed wallets and develop the rule.
- **Selection period:** establish accounting eligibility and select candidates.
- **Untouched future period:** evaluate the frozen candidate list and copy strategy.

With little data, use discovery/selection together and a genuinely prospective test. That is better than pretending two retrospective halves are out-of-sample.

### E. Your copy simulation uses prints where it needs executable transactions

“Enter one second later on a buy print” leaves fatal ambiguities:

- Is that the next buy after one second?
- What happens when there is no next print?
- Are you getting that transaction’s average price, marginal price, or the state after it?
- Is your order small enough not to alter execution?
- Does the exit also include your detection and transaction delay?

A subsequent print can be much later and need not be a price you could obtain.

**Fix:** Simulate an order against the relevant curve/pool state at the follower’s assumed landing time, with size, fees, slippage limits, and failure rules. If the historical state cannot be recovered, label the result a **price proxy**, not copyable P&L.

Your measured one-second **send latency** may not include observation, RPC delivery, decision time, and landing. Measure that complete path. Three slots are not a fixed wall-clock guarantee.

### F. “Nobody to sell to” is not the same as “cannot sell” on pump.fun

A bonding curve or AMM can buy your tokens without another human placing a contemporaneous buy.

No subsequent sell print does not prove zero executable value. Conversely, a displayed price does not prove that your position can exit at that price.

**Fix:** Separate:

1. no observed exit;
2. no available execution route;
3. available route with negligible liquidation proceeds.

Also distinguish dead tokens from tokens that migrated. A signature-cap check alone does not establish lifecycle completeness across all routes.

### G. Capital constraints are missing

A follower with €52 cannot independently fund every attractive historical trade.

You need a chronological portfolio simulation including:

- concurrent positions;
- cash locked in inventory;
- skipped trades when cash runs out;
- minimum practical order size;
- transaction costs and account creation;
- failed transactions;
- reserve SOL for exits.

**Do not turn a strategy that requires €2,000 of overlapping inventory into a €52 strategy by reporting percentage returns per trade.** Test €52 separately from the “few hundred euros” question.

## 2. Correct inventory treatment—and the cheapest defensible version

For an entity over a defined window:

\[
P\&L =
\text{trading proceeds}
-\text{trading expenditure}
-\text{costs}
+V_{\text{closing inventory}}
-V_{\text{opening inventory}}
\]

External contributions and distributions must be accounted for separately. Internal transfers between entity addresses cancel. SOL/WSOL conversions must not create gains.

For economic P&L, inventory value should be **net executable liquidation value**, not last-trade price:

- current relevant curve/pool reserves;
- your actual position size;
- sell fees and execution costs;
- available routes;
- any token restrictions.

On tokens with no viable liquidation route, value is zero.

### Cheap implementation

Maintain two results:

**1. Conservative cash-recovery result**

For inventory bought within your observation window, charge the entire purchase expenditure immediately and give remaining inventory zero value.

This is a useful conservative measure. But call it **zero-marked trading P&L**, not realised P&L.

Crucially:

> If purchases are already counted as cash outflows, do not subtract “−100% on unsold positions” again.

That would double-count the loss.

**2. Liquidation-adjusted result for finalists**

For the few candidates that survive, batch-query current token balances and the relevant curve/pool state. Estimate net proceeds from closing remaining positions.

Do not spend credits pricing every worthless tail holding in the initial screen.

### The expensive trap is opening inventory, not closing inventory

Zero-marking closing inventory makes results more conservative. Ignoring opening inventory can make them wildly optimistic.

For every token sold, establish its source:

- purchased during the window;
- present at the start;
- transferred in;
- minted/allocated/airdropped.

Unknown acquisition economics mean the trading-profit claim is unresolved.

A free allocation can generate genuine income. It is **not evidence that an ordinary follower could profit by buying**. Report allocation/launcher income separately from secondary-market trading.

Also: current balances cannot reconstruct inventory at a historical split date. Historical boundaries require the ledger or an appropriate historical state source.

## 3. Wallet selection: a better cheap seed

**Use an outcome-blind, time-based sample of pump.fun activity, collected before evaluating the wallets’ returns.**

A workable procedure:

1. Predeclare several calendar intervals or randomly selected time buckets, spread across hours and days.
2. Collect pump.fun buy activity in those intervals, independently of eventual token survival.
3. Identify the economic buyers.
4. Deduplicate addresses.
5. Select a reproducible random subset using a fixed hash rule.
6. Freeze that seed list before calculating profitability.

Do not seed from “most profitable,” “most active in our tokens,” or “buyers of surviving tokens.”

### Be honest about what this samples

Sampling transactions and then wallets overrepresents active wallets. Deduplication alone does not remove that inclusion bias.

If you enumerate all buyers in the chosen intervals and randomly select among those distinct buyers, your population is **wallets active during those intervals**, not all pump.fun wallets ever.

That is a defensible population. A supposedly unbiased universal wallet sample is not necessary.

### Budget control

Use staged spending:

- Cheap discovery and signature enumeration.
- Fixed-window accounting on a modest random cohort.
- Deep reconciliation on preliminary candidates.
- Detailed execution reconstruction only on finalists.
- Reserve a material share of credits for the untouched test.

Check the actual credit costs of all endpoints; do not budget only parsed transactions.

If some wallets exceed the history budget, apply a predeclared coverage rule and report exclusions. Do not simply take their most recent 200 transactions and call that their record.

Keep your existing ~130-token cohort as a separate convenience sample. It is useful for tooling and hypotheses, not prevalence estimates.

## 4. The false-winner mechanism—and the guard before ranking

There are two different winner factories. Both need explicit guards.

### Factory 1: The sell-only distribution wallet

An operator:

1. acquires or receives tokens in wallet A;
2. transfers them to B;
3. sells from B.

Your reader sees B receiving SOL but does not see B paying for the tokens. B becomes an apparently extraordinary persistent trader. Repeat across launches and the “persistence” looks stronger.

History truncation produces the same effect without any deliberate concealment.

### Pre-ranking guard: inventory-source reconciliation

**No entity enters the trading-profit ranking until every material disposal has an accounted-for acquisition or opening inventory value, and the token ledger reconciles.**

For unexplained receipts:

- trace them within a limited budget;
- classify them separately as allocation/transfer-dependent;
- or exclude the entity from the clean-trading ranking.

Do this **before sorting by profit**. Otherwise the largest accounting holes become the candidates receiving your attention.

Clustering only the previously observed seed addresses will not suffice: the funding or acquisition wallet may not be in the seed cohort.

### Factory 2: The lottery winner selected retrospectively

Among thousands of addresses, someone:

- buys one enormous winner;
- has a lucky run;
- trades within a profitable coordinated campaign;
- or happens to look good under your chosen split.

If you inspect enough thresholds and variations, something will look persistent.

### Pre-ranking guard: freeze the experiment

Before computing rankings, freeze:

- seed population;
- accounting eligibility;
- entity rules;
- calendar boundaries;
- candidate-selection rule;
- number of candidates;
- follower sizing and exit policy;
- final evaluation metric.

Then evaluate the selected list once on future data.

**A minimum trade count does not solve this.** Neither does “profitable in both halves” if you used both halves to find the winners.

Report dependence on the largest token and campaign. Ten wallets on the same launch are not ten independent confirmations. Use a third prospective period if you have already inspected and tuned against the nominal holdout.

## 5. What persistent winners would actually look like

Search for mechanisms, not just green totals.

| Possible mechanism | Observable signature | Implication for copying |
|---|---|---|
| **Launcher/insider distribution** | Inventory received before public trading; creator/funder links; repeated very early holdings; sales into subsequent inflow | Profit may be real, but follower cannot reproduce acquisition terms |
| **Coordinated launch operator** | Related wallets enter the same launches within slots; repeated funding links; synchronized exits; shared destinations for proceeds | One operation, not independent traders; copying may supply their exit liquidity |
| **Latency or ordering advantage** | Very short holding periods; profits concentrated within slots; repeated profitable ordering around other transactions; unusually high tips/priority fees | A one-second-late follower probably misses the edge |
| **Cross-venue arbitrage** | Near-simultaneous offsetting swaps, little sustained inventory, profit tied to price discrepancies | Not a directional copy signal; copying one leg can be harmful |
| **Repeatable token selection and execution** | Paid acquisitions with reconciled costs; gains across multiple unrelated launches and calendar periods; exits actually achieved; limited reliance on one windfall | The most plausible candidate for your proposed follower |
| **Allocation or fee income** | Token receipts or protocol payments not explained by ordinary purchases | Real income, but a different business from copy trading |

For the last plausible copyable category, demand one additional signature:

> **Positive incremental returns remain after the follower’s entry delay, exit delay, costs, and bankroll constraints—not merely before them.**

That requires more than the leader’s profitability. Look for:

- an edge that develops over minutes rather than being consumed within the first second;
- enough exit liquidity after the leader sells;
- profits spread across independent opportunities;
- a tolerable inventory tail;
- positive follower performance in the untouched period.

These are hypotheses to test, not proof from a visual pattern.

### Fix the benchmark too

“Any buy on the same tokens” conditions on the leader’s token selection. It tests **timing within those tokens**, not the entire value of following that wallet.

Use two controls:

1. **Same-token, comparable lifecycle/time buys:** does this wallet time better?
2. **Contemporaneous buys from the eligible pump.fun universe:** does the wallet’s combined token selection and timing add value?

Apply identical follower latency, sizing, exit rules, costs, and inventory treatment to both.

## The minimum study worth running

**Outcome-blind seed → reconciled entity ledger → fixed calendar selection → frozen candidates → prospective, bankroll-constrained follower test.**

Your first deliverable should be a reconciliation table, not a wallet leaderboard. If you cannot distinguish *bought cheaply* from *received elsewhere*, or *observable print* from *executable exit*, more history will make the wrong answer more persuasive—not more correct.
