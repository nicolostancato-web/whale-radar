**Your search is well designed to ask whether generic public signals predict short-horizon returns. It is poorly designed to ask whether you possess an economically valuable advantage. Those are different questions.**

The recurring result may not mean you keep missing a sufficiently clever signal. It may mean you keep searching for forecasting advantages in a setting where **execution, access, and financing determine who can monetize them**—and those are precisely the advantages you do not have.

Two corrections matter before interpreting the evidence:

- **0.0203% is about one tenth of 0.20%, not one fifth.** The pairs effect needs roughly a tenfold improvement in gross return, a 90% reduction in costs, or a combination.
- Averaging a rule and its reverse to minus costs is a useful accounting check, **not proof that the measurements are sound**. With symmetric linear P&L, gross returns cancel algebraically. Look-ahead bias, unrealistic fills, incorrect timestamps, and unsuitable controls can all survive that check. I am not alleging those errors; I am rejecting that particular certification.

## 1. What your instrument cannot see

There are three different blind spots: information you discard, effects your aggregation cancels, and opportunities your economic model does not represent.

| Structural blind spot | What becomes invisible or misrepresented |
|---|---|
| **Hourly candles discard event order and executable prices.** | Whether the signal precedes the price move; whether a quote was actually available; whether both legs could execute together; whether the opportunity lasted milliseconds or minutes. Identical candles can contain very different executable opportunities. |
| **Pooling assumes some transferability across markets and states.** | An effect confined to a particular mechanism, venue, or regime can be diluted. Opposite effects in different states can cancel. A zero family centre does not establish that every economically defined subgroup has zero expectancy. |
| **A fixed holding period substitutes the clock for the mechanism.** | An effect realized at a specific resolution, convergence, settlement, or flow event may be understated—or completely missed—by hourly entry/exit returns. |
| **A fresh round trip is charged to every forecast.** | The incremental value of a signal to someone already holding inventory, already needing to trade, or able to net trades across a portfolio. Avoiding a bad trade can be valuable without supporting a profitable standalone trade. |
| **Directional return is treated as the source of payment.** | Compensation for providing a service, balance sheet, immediacy, or operational reliability. These need not appear as predictable candle returns at all. |

**Demanding that costs be covered is not the mistake.** That is the necessary economic constraint. The potential mistake is assuming every monetization takes the form of an independently opened and closed position.

But an important distinction follows:

> Something can be invisible to your instrument without being accessible to you.

A subsecond opportunity may be invisible in hourly candles and unavailable at your latency. Inventory-dependent value may be invisible in standalone backtests and unavailable without inventory. Discovering that blind spot does not rescue the business case.

Also, your class-level tests support narrower conclusions than “families closed.” They strongly weaken the case for **broad, transferable effects under the tested definitions**. They do not prove the absence of sparse, mechanism-specific effects. Conversely, that logical opening is not a good reason to resume unrestricted subgroup hunting.

## 2. What the pairs result actually says

It says there is **a candidate gross predictive effect associated with the linkage**, under your measurement scheme. It does not yet establish an independently replicated, executable edge.

In particular, 435 pairs are not necessarily 435 independent observations. Shared assets and shared episodes can move many pair statistics together. The centre shift deserves attention, but its uncertainty must reflect that dependence. Shuffled controls must also preserve relevant volatility and regime structure.

Taking the gross estimate at face value, the economics are stark:

- Gross: **2.03 basis points**
- Cost: **20 basis points**
- Net: **−17.97 basis points**

There are only a few algebraic ways out, and none follows from the centre shift:

**Longer holding.** This works only if expected cumulative convergence continues growing faster than costs such as funding, borrow, and exit friction. A one-hour gross estimate does not tell you that. The effect could accumulate, saturate, or reverse. You need the cumulative response curve, not an assumption that ten times longer means ten times more return.

**Different selection.** The current average does not reveal whether economically identifiable cases exceed 20 basis points. A stronger subgroup could exist. Selecting it retrospectively from the best observed outcomes, however, would recreate exactly the winner-hunting problem you have avoided.

**Lower-cost execution.** On the same return/notional convention, total all-in cost would need to fall below 2.03 basis points. Across four equally weighted legs, that means roughly **0.51 basis points per leg**, including fees, spread, slippage, and adverse selection—not merely the advertised fee. That is a concrete access requirement, not a new signal.

**Trade netting.** If your backtest charges four legs independently to every pair, a portfolio might require less turnover because overlapping positions cancel. But gross return must then be recomputed on the actual net portfolio too. You cannot retain all standalone pair profits while cancelling only their costs.

One accounting prerequisite: verify that pair returns and four-leg costs use **the same capital/notional denominator**. “Four legs” alone does not establish the correct percentage cost.

Is 2.03 basis points “the market’s price of that inefficiency”? **You cannot infer that.** It could be residual compensation available to lower-cost participants, an incomplete adjustment, or partly a measurement artifact.

The defensible conclusion is narrower: **the measured effect is not monetizable through your measured implementation.** Longer holding or another venue is not a demonstrated escape hatch.

## 3. The shared assumption behind the rut

For the trading families you describe, the shared assumption is:

> **There is a sufficiently large, portable return forecast in public market data that an outsider can monetize through fresh, ordinary trades without a corresponding advantage in execution, access, financing, or inventory.**

Price patterns, attention, rankings, linked pairs, positioning, and market-size tiers change the predictor. They mostly leave the economic architecture untouched.

That is the rut: **you vary the informational story while holding your disadvantaged route to payment constant.**

Your “small markets, same fee, less competition” hypothesis illustrates it particularly well. It changes presumed competition, but retains the premise that less competition must manifest as a larger candle-predictable return accessible through your existing execution.

The more fundamental question is not “what predicts price?” It is:

**What does the counterparty pay you for, and why can someone better equipped not supply it more cheaply?**

A return prediction can answer that question, but only if it is proprietary enough, durable enough, or executable cheaply enough. “A real statistical effect exists” does not answer it.

Your on-chain payout measurements expose a related problem: **worldwide payout is neither your addressable revenue nor provider profit.** An accurately measured reward pool can still have virtually no economically accessible share for your setup.

## 4. Can this search honestly be declared unable to succeed?

**The exact search you describe can reasonably be stopped now.** You have repeated evidence against the proposition that broad public-data forecasts, traded through your current access, clear your costs.

That is a decision under evidence—not a theorem that no clever person can ever earn money starting with €52.

There are two constraints, and they should not be conflated:

1. **Negative unit economics.** More capital does not turn a 2.03-basis-point gross effect against 20-basis-point costs into a profitable trade. It scales the loss unless it changes your execution or access.
2. **Negligible absolute economics.** Even a genuinely profitable strategy may be commercially irrelevant on €52. A hypothetical 1% net monthly return is €0.52 per month. That is arithmetic, not a forecast.

So **“capital or product” is not quite the correct dichotomy**. Capital helps only if it unlocks different economics or scales an already positive edge. A product is one way to earn payment for useful work; employment and services are others. None requires first discovering a tradeable market inefficiency.

My plain conclusion:

**Stop treating another public-signal family as the default next experiment. Your evidence no longer supports that use of time.** Reopening the search should require a concrete change in execution economics, access, or the source of payment—not merely another predictor.

You have not established that markets contain no opportunities. You have established a strong case that **your current search asks you to discover an advantage while excluding most of the mechanisms that would let you get paid for it.**
